Pre-published · preprint live

Emergency preparedness planning: lessons from COVID-19 and the opioid crisis in rural Ohio

First author · Westlund BR, Sibley AL, Colston DC, Devadas J, Enderle MN, Piscalko HM, Miller WC, Go VF

The COVID-19 pandemic amplified an already severe overdose crisis, but its effect on rural emergency preparedness was largely undocumented. Drawing on 52 semi-structured interviews with people who use drugs and local stakeholders across two Appalachian Ohio counties, this study found that existing preparedness plans were outdated, unfamiliar to staff, and built for short-term disasters rather than sustained public health emergencies — with almost no consideration of the needs of people who use drugs. Service disruptions, inconsistent communication, and heightened stigma compounded the harm, though communities responded with peer distribution networks and rapid telehealth adoption.

52 interviews Thematic analysis Dedoose Triple/double-coded NIH Rural Opioid Initiative
DOI: 10.64898/2026.07.30.26359388 ↗
// WHY THIS MATTERS FOR DESIGN

The central finding is a design failure: plans existed, but the people meant to use them couldn't find them, didn't recognize them, and weren't represented in them. Systems fail the same way products do — when they're built without the end user in the room.

Accepted for presentation · SIRC 2026

When is the juice worth the squeeze? Costs versus benefits of implementation strategy tracking

First author · Westlund B, Powell B, Dang HLT, Cao TVA, Nguyen HN, Nguyen KN, Sripaipan T, Rossi SL, Miller WC, Go VF, Bartels SM

Tracking how implementation strategies actually get used ranges from coding every meeting recording to a retrospective skim of program reports — with wildly different costs. A structured literature review plus a recorded, facilitated researcher debate surfaced three core tensions: rigor vs. feasibility, prospective vs. retrospective documentation, and researcher- vs. implementer-led tracking. Effort ranged from under 15 minutes per log entry to more than 11,000 person-hours across a multi-year study. The result is a practical decision framework for choosing tracking intensity based on setting, resource capacity, study design, and purpose.

Structured literature review Facilitated debate method Consensus building Decision framework
// WHY THIS MATTERS FOR DESIGN

This is fundamentally a research-ops question — how much measurement is worth its cost — and the output is a decision tool, not a finding. Knowing when not to over-instrument is as useful in a product org as it is in an implementation trial.

In draft · manuscript in preparation

Healthcare providers' own use in Vietnam: A socioecological analysis of motivations, barriers, and facilitators to drinking

First author · Secondary qualitative analysis, EBAI trial

Healthcare providers are expected to counsel patients on alcohol use, yet their own drinking is rarely studied — despite evidence that it shapes how willing they are to intervene. Analyzing 16 post-training interviews from ART clinics across three regions of Vietnam through the socioecological model, this study found that barriers to reducing drinking were almost never individual. Providers rarely described craving or dependence; instead they described social obligation, workplace hierarchy, and the real cost of refusing a superior's invitation. Where leadership set a non-drinking norm, reduction became far easier to sustain.

16 in-depth interviews Framework Method NVivo 14 Socioecological model 25% double-coded
// WHY THIS MATTERS FOR DESIGN

A textbook case of why you don't design for stated individual intent. Every provider said reducing was up to them; the data said it was governed by hierarchy and social cost. The intervention target moves from the person to the environment.
