Emergency preparedness planning: lessons from COVID-19 and the opioid crisis in rural Ohio

Westlund BR, Sibley AL, Colston DC, Devadas J, Enderle MN, Piscalko HM, Miller WC, Go VF

The COVID-19 pandemic made an already severe overdose crisis worse, but its effect on rural emergency preparedness was largely undocumented. Drawing on 52 semi-structured interviews with people who use drugs and local stakeholders in two Appalachian Ohio counties, we found that existing preparedness plans were outdated, unfamiliar to staff, and built for short-term disasters rather than sustained public health emergencies, with almost no consideration of people who use drugs. Service disruptions, inconsistent communication, and stigma made things worse, though communities responded with peer distribution networks and quick adoption of telehealth.

52 interviews · Thematic analysis · Dedoose · Double and triple coding · NIH Rural Opioid Initiative

DOI: 10.64898/2026.07.30.26359388

Why it matters for design

The central finding describes a design failure: the plans existed, but the people meant to use them couldn't find them, didn't recognize them, and weren't represented in them. Products fail the same way when they're built without their users.

When is the juice worth the squeeze? Costs versus benefits of implementation strategy tracking

Westlund B, Powell B, Dang HLT, Cao TVA, Nguyen HN, Nguyen KN, Sripaipan T, Rossi SL, Miller WC, Go VF, Bartels SM

Tracking how implementation strategies get used can mean anything from coding every meeting recording to skimming program reports after the fact, at very different costs. A structured literature review and a recorded, facilitated debate among researchers surfaced three core tensions: rigor versus feasibility, prospective versus retrospective documentation, and researcher-led versus implementer-led tracking. Effort ranged from under 15 minutes per log entry to more than 11,000 person-hours across a multi-year study. The result is a practical framework for choosing how intensively to track, based on setting, resources, study design, and purpose.

Structured literature review · Facilitated debate · Consensus building · Decision framework

Why it matters for design

This is a research operations question: how much measurement is worth its cost. The output is a decision tool, and knowing when not to over-instrument matters as much in a product team as in an implementation trial.

Healthcare providers' own use in Vietnam: a socioecological analysis of motivations, barriers, and facilitators to drinking

Secondary qualitative analysis, EBAI trial

Healthcare providers are expected to counsel patients about alcohol, yet their own drinking is rarely studied, even though it shapes how willing they are to intervene. Analyzing 16 post-training interviews from ART clinics in three regions of Vietnam through the socioecological model, we found that barriers to drinking less were rarely individual. Providers seldom described craving or dependence. They described social obligation, workplace hierarchy, and the real cost of turning down a superior's invitation. Where leadership set a non-drinking norm, cutting back was much easier to sustain.

16 in-depth interviews · Framework Method · NVivo 14 · Socioecological model · 25% double-coded

Upskilling Everyone: a multipronged strategy to foster generative AI literacy in faculty and students simultaneously

Merino YM, Westlund BR, Rice DK

Most generative AI training in higher education targets either faculty or students. This paper describes a strategy that builds AI literacy in both groups at once, so the people designing coursework and the people completing it develop a shared understanding of what these tools can and can't do. My current interview study picks up the same question from the design side.
